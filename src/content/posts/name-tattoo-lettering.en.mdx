# Name tattoo lettering: the useful checks happen at final size

A name can look balanced on a phone and cramped on skin. The reason is simple: a preview is usually enlarged, flat, and viewed straight on. A tattoo is seen at its intended physical size, on a curved surface, while the body moves. A font picker helps you compare directions; it cannot decide whether a particular word will remain easy to read at a particular scale.

This guide is for choosing and discussing a lettering direction—not for replacing a tattoo artist’s drawing or stencil process.

## Start with the actual word, not a font category

“Script” or “Old English” describes a broad family, not how your exact name will behave. The letters in _Mia_ create different spacing problems from _William_: repeated vertical strokes, round counters, joins, capitals, and flourishes all change the silhouette.

Type the exact spelling, capitalization, accents, and punctuation you want. If the name uses a language you do not speak, verify the spelling and diacritics with a fluent speaker before the appointment. Ask another person to read the draft without being told what it says. Tattooing 101’s lettering guidance makes the same practical point: ornamental letterforms can be misread, and spelling should be checked before the design is applied ([Tattooing 101: styles and lettering](https://tattooing101.com/learn/styles/)).

## Compare letter shapes and the spaces between them

When comparing two previews, do not judge only the outer silhouette. Look inside and between the letters:

- **Counters:** the open spaces in letters such as “a,” “e,” and “o.” A tight counter can close visually when the design is reduced.
- **Joins:** in connected script, check where one letter enters the next. Decorative swashes should not obscure the basic reading order.
- **Repeated strokes:** pairs such as “ll,” “nn,” and “mm” need clear separation. Equal mathematical spacing is not always equal-looking spacing, so an artist may adjust it optically.
- **Line contrast:** very thin hairlines next to heavy downstrokes may need simplification at a small scale.
- **Negative space:** the uninked gaps are part of the drawing. If those gaps disappear in a small print, the design needs more room or less detail.

Tattooing 101’s design guide discusses spacing and “skin breaks” as practical ways to stop dense linework from reading as one dark mass ([design techniques](https://tattooing101.com/learn/techniques/design/)). There is no universal minimum size that makes every font safe: the word, lettering style, placement, skin, and artist’s technique all matter.

## Print at the intended size before you decide

A large screen preview hides crowding. Ask for a paper proof at the size you are actually considering. View it at a normal conversational distance, not only with your face close to the page. Check whether every letter is distinguishable and whether the overall word fits the available area without squeezing its proportions.

For a curved placement, inspect how the baseline follows the body rather than trusting a flat mockup. A name on the inner forearm, collarbone, ribs, or ankle will meet a different curve and movement pattern. Ask the artist to position the stencil while you stand or rest naturally, then check it from the main viewing angle and from the side. If the design wraps, make sure the ends do not disappear around the limb in the posture in which you expect to see it.

## Use a digital preview for comparison, not approval

A font preview is useful for narrowing choices: for example, compare a restrained serif with a connected script and a heavier display style using the same text. It can also help communicate whether you want an intimate handwritten feel, a formal inscription, or a bold graphic word.

It cannot show how a tattoo will heal, how your artist will draw the letters, or how the exact lines will sit on your skin. Exported PNG/SVG files are references, not shop-ready transfer instructions. Final line weight, spacing, size, and placement belong in a conversation with the artist.

## A five-minute appointment checklist

Bring the exact text and ask the artist to review these points:

1. Is the spelling, capitalization, and accenting correct?
2. Can a new reader identify every letter without a hint?
3. Are counters and gaps still open in a print at the intended size?
4. Does the line of text sit naturally on the body in a relaxed posture?
5. Does the artist want to redraw or simplify any flourishes before making the stencil?

The strongest reference is not the most ornate one. It is the one that communicates the lettering you like while leaving the artist enough room to make a clear, workable design for your body.
